# What the kit said

This example exists because it is the run that does NOT produce a template.
That is the kit working, not the kit failing.

---

## The count, then the stop

> I counted. After the parts that change per job separate out — the client, the
> work, the price, the date — there are **fourteen words** of wording that stay
> the same every time. Roughly 88% of this document changes per job.
>
> **That is not enough repeatable wording to be worth locking.** A template
> built from this would be four blanks and the word "Per." You would spend
> more time filling the form than typing the email, and I am not going to hand
> you that and call it a product.
>
> Two ways forward:
>
> 1. **Show me a different document.** For a mechanical contractor, the ones
>    that usually repeat enough are a proposal or quote, a service agreement,
>    or a payment-terms letter. If you have one of those, that is the one
>    worth building.
> 2. **Tell me to build it anyway**, and I will — but I will mark it a thin
>    template and flag every sentence I had to invent, because inventing your
>    wording is exactly what makes a document sound like it came from a
>    machine.

**Sal:** Nah, you're right, that's just an email.

## But the check list still had something to say

> One thing before we move on, and then I will drop it. I ran your email
> against what a change order usually carries. Not to sell you a template —
> you just told me you do not want one. Four things are missing, and three of
> them are the ones that turn into arguments over money:
>
> - **The new total.** You wrote "add $6,400." A change order that shows only
>   the delta hides the running total; one that shows only the total hides the
>   size of the change. The ones that hold up show both.
> - **Schedule impact.** Even "no change to the completion date" — silence
>   here is what the dispute is about later.
> - **Approval.** Nothing says work does not start until Rich says yes, in
>   writing. "Let me know" is not that.
> - **What it references.** No mention of the original contract or its date.
>
> Those four are worth more to you than a template of that email would have
> been.

**Sal:** ...say that again about the total.

## What he came back with

Sal came back the next day with a proposal — the document he sends when he is
bidding a job, three pages, largely the same every time.

> **412 words** of wording that stays the same, about 22% variable. That is a
> real template, and it is the one that was worth building.

The change-order gaps went in as a one-page note he keeps beside the proposal
template. The kit did not build him a change-order template, and it should not
have.

## Why this example ships with the kit

Every kit in this line can produce something. That is not the same as every
kit being worth what it costs. Doc Factory has seven named refusal conditions,
and this is the most common one: **the document you brought is not a template,
it is an email.**

The refusal costs the kit a sale of a bad template and buys it the trust to
sell a good one the next day. That is the trade, deliberately.
