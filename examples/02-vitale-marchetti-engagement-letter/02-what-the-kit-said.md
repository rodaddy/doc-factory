# What the kit said

## The refusal fired before anything got built

> I have your letter. Before I lock any wording, one line stopped me.
>
> This sentence is in the part I was about to lock as permanent wording:
>
> > "As we discussed following the situation with the prior bookkeeper at
> > Grasso, we will also perform a one-time review of the 2025 general
> > ledger."
>
> That is Anthony Grasso's business, and it refers to something that happened
> at his company. If I lock it, that sentence goes out on every engagement
> letter you send to every other client from now on.
>
> Tell me what should go there instead: a blank I ask you for each time
> ("anything extra we agreed to do for this client"), or nothing at all.

**Gina:** Oh God. Take it out entirely — that's a one-off, that should never
have been in the file I copy from.

> Removed. It is not a field and it is not locked wording; it is gone. If a
> future letter needs a one-off, you will tell me and I will add it for that
> letter only.

That is the whole point of the refusal. She had been copying that letter for
five months.

## Then the count

> After the fields separate out, your letter has **287 words** of wording that
> stays the same every time, and about 31% of it changes per client. That is a
> good template — well past the line where it is worth locking.

## The gap report

> I ran your letter against the checks for an engagement letter. That is the
> highest-risk kind of document in here, so the list is long. Eight of eleven
> are covered by your own wording. Three are not.
>
> **1. There is no sentence saying what is NOT included.** You say what you
> will do. You do not say what falls outside it. That is the most common
> omission in this kind of letter and it is the one that turns into an argument
> in month nine, when someone assumes something was covered.
>
> **2. There is no term and no termination.** The letter does not say how long
> this runs or how either side ends it. Right now it reads as open-ended in
> both directions.
>
> **3. There is no payment-terms consequence.** You say the fee is billed on
> the first. You do not say what happens if it is not paid.
>
> Three things I want to be clear about. I am not telling you what your letter
> should say. I am telling you what is not in it. This is your document and
> your profession, and anything with legal weight is a question for your
> attorney, not for me. What I can do is hold a blank for wording you give me,
> or flag the gap on every letter until you fill it.

**Gina:** The scope one is real. We had exactly that fight last year. Give me
a minute.

*(Gina writes two sentences. The kit locks them word for word — her wording,
not the kit's.)*

**Gina:** Termination, I need to ask our attorney. Leave it flagged.
Payment terms, leave it, I don't want to be the firm that charges interest.

> Recorded, three different ways:
>
> - **Scope boundary** — your two sentences are now locked wording. They go
>   out on every engagement letter.
> - **Term and termination** — stays FLAGGED. Every letter you build will show
>   it on the verification page until you tell me it is handled. It will not
>   block you; it will not let you forget either.
> - **Payment-terms consequence** — recorded as deliberately left out. I will
>   not mention it again.

## The first real one

> Who is the next engagement letter going to?

**Gina:** New client, Bay Ridge Dental Group, starting next month.

*(Eleven questions. The verification page comes up carrying one FLAG —
"no term and termination clause; you asked me to keep flagging this" — and
the provenance list showing that the scope paragraph is L3 from the March 3
letter and the boundary sentences are L4, her own wording from today. She
reads the flag, accepts it knowingly, and clicks.)*

The receipt records that she saw the flag and accepted it anyway. That is what
the record is for.
