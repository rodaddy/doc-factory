# What the kit said — Kowalczyk Title Agency

The conversation, in the kit's own words. Four things happen here that do not
happen in examples 1 through 3: the owner is unsure which kind of document she
has, a whole check list gets dropped, a promise nearly gets locked forever, and
the most useful finding in the letter is one no check list contained.

---

## 1. Classifying it, when the owner is not sure

> **Kit:** What is this document for? Is it (a) agreeing to do work for
> someone, (b) telling a client where things stand, (c) changing the scope or
> price of work already agreed, (d) asking for money, (e) offering a price for
> work not yet agreed, or (f) something else?
>
> **Deirdre:** It's (b), telling them where things stand. But it also has our
> premium on it, so I guess (d)? And it tells them the three things they have
> to clear.
>
> **Kit:** Then it is both, and that is normal — plenty of real documents are
> two of these at once. I will check it both ways. Before I do, here is what
> the second one makes me ask, so you can tell me if it is beside the point:
> because it states a price, I would check it the way I check a bill — is there
> a due date on it, and is there a way to pay. Does anyone actually pay you
> from this letter, or does that money move somewhere else?
>
> **Deirdre:** Nobody pays me from this letter. The premium goes on the closing
> statement and comes out of the closing. If I put a due date on it my attorneys
> would think I lost my mind.
>
> **Kit:** Then I am dropping the bill checks entirely and I will not raise
> them again on this template. Noted in your checks file with your reason, so
> nobody wonders in a year why a letter with a price on it is not being checked
> like an invoice.

**What just did not happen:** three of the seven findings in the gap report
were about to be "your fee notice has no due date and no payment route." On a
title agency's clear-to-close letter that is nonsense, and it would have burned
the one moment the customer was paying attention.

---

## 2. The counts, and the gate that passed

> **Kit:** I counted. 221 words of this letter stay the same every time, and
> 29% of it changes per client. That is well past the floor — this is a
> document worth locking. Eleven fields. Building it.

---

## 3. The promise that was one step from becoming policy

> **Kit:** Stop — one sentence in here is a promise, and locking it promises it
> to every client from now on, in writing:
>
> *"...which we will run at no additional charge within ten days of closing."*
>
> Is that your standard for everyone, or was it something you did for this one?
>
> **Deirdre:** Ten days? We did that for Ron because he's Ron. That's not our
> policy. God.
>
> **Kit:** Then it comes out of the locked wording. I will ask you each time
> whether this letter should include it, and it defaults to not included.
> Recorded in your sources file as a one-off, so it cannot wander back in from
> the same letter later.
>
> While we are here, the other side of the same coin — this sentence I am
> locking as-is unless you tell me otherwise:
>
> *"Wire instructions will be transmitted separately and by telephone
> confirmation only; we do not accept changed wire instructions by email under
> any circumstances."*
>
> **Deirdre:** That one goes on everything. That one is the whole reason we
> haven't been wired out of business.
>
> **Kit:** Locked. L5.

**Why this is the example.** Both sentences are generic — neither one names a
client, so the client-facts gate does not see either of them. One of them is
the most valuable sentence in the letter and one of them is a favour that was
about to become a published policy. The only thing that tells them apart is
asking, and asking is now a stop the kit is required to make.

---

## 4. The gap report — including the one no list contained

> **Kit:** I ran your letter against the checks for a status letter. Most of it
> is covered. Three things are not.
>
> **The one that matters most:** three of your deadlines are measured from the
> closing — "at or prior to closing," "forty-eight hours prior to the scheduled
> closing," "within ten days of closing" — and the closing date is not written
> anywhere in this letter. Ron knows it. His secretary opening the mail on
> Wednesday does not. What is it, and I will put it in the Re: line where it
> is impossible to miss?
>
> **Deirdre:** ...Nobody has ever said that to me.
>
> **Kit:** Second: item 1, the mortgage satisfaction, does not say who is
> getting it. Items 2 and 3 imply the seller. Third: the premium is stated but
> the endorsements point at an annexed rate sheet, so the total is not in the
> letter — that may be exactly how you want it, and if so I will stop asking.
>
> I am not telling you what your letter should say — that is yours, and
> anything with legal weight is a question for your counsel. I am telling you
> what is not in it.
>
> **Deirdre:** Put the closing date in. Leave the rest.
>
> **Kit:** Done. The other two are marked as your decision and will not come up
> again.

---

## What Deirdre got out of one sitting

Her own wording locked, so she never retypes it. A standing promise caught
before it became policy. A gap in a letter she has been sending for nine years,
named in one sentence. And nothing goes out of her office until she has read it
and clicked to say so, with the date recorded.

Angie in her office still writes every one of these. She writes them in about
four minutes now instead of forty, and she is the one who clicks the button.
